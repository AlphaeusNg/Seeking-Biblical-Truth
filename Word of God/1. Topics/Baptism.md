The essentiality of Baptism required for [[Salvation]] hinges on the interpretation of these verses:
- For -> [[Acts 2_38]], [[Acts 22_16]], [[Mark 16_16]], [[1 Peter 3_20-21]], [[John 3_5]], [[Gal 3_27]]
- "Against" -> [[John 3_16]], [[Acts 16_31]], [[Eph 2_8-9]], [[Eph 5_25-27]]
	- Not necessarily contradicts Baptism, but more of the idea that [[Belief]] or [[Faith]] in Christ is the axiom for salvation, and Baptism fits into that framework via being an "outward sign of an inward faith" [[Rom 4]] of the parallel to Abraham's justification. Therefore salvation occurs at belief rather than baptism.
	- [[Eph 5_25-27]]: 25 Husbands, love your wives, as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her, 26 that he might **sanctify her, having cleansed her by the washing of water with the word**, 27 so that he might present the church to himself in splendor, without spot or wrinkle or any such thing, that she might be holy and without blemish.
		- The "washing of water" with the word is the *thing* that cleanses her unto sanctification (set apart, holy). Water more of a symbol of cleansing rather than the link to baptism?

### Seems to not be considered a literal part in the [[Gospel]]
When I read [[1 Cor 1]], I was surprised to find out 2 things of how Paul views Baptism in v14-16:
	*14 I thank God that ==I baptized none of you== except Crispus and Gaius, 15 so that no one may say that you were baptized in my name. 16 (I did baptize also the household of Stephanas. Beyond that, I do not know whether I baptized anyone else.) 17 For ==Christ did not send me to baptize but to preach the gospel==, and not with words of eloquent wisdom, lest the cross of Christ be emptied of its power.*
1. He separates Baptism from the [[Gospel]]. If the Gospel is the power of Salvation to those who believe ([[Rom 1_16]]), and Baptism is a necessary component of that, Paul would be contradicting himself by not fulfilling that requirement.
	1. Granted we recognize that the point Paul was making was in addressing the divisions that arose in Corinthians and Paul was glad that by not baptizing them, he reduced one source of division, yet there is still valid theological implications of his statements.
2. If Baptism is to be understood as *essential* for salvation, Paul is literally saying:
	*14 I thank God that ==I <u>saved</u> none of you== except Crispus and Gaius, 15 so that no one may say that you were <u>saved</u> in my name. 16 (I did <u>save</u> also the household of Stephanas. Beyond that, I do not know whether I <u>saved</u> anyone else.) 17 For ==Christ did not send me to <u>save</u> but to preach the gospel==, and not with words of eloquent wisdom, lest the cross of Christ be emptied of its power.*

While Baptism does have a "strong salvific scent" due to what Jesus commands in [[Matt 28]] and the other verses above that seems to tie salvation directly to it, perhaps the most faithful and biblical way to understand it is that it occurs as a *result* of saving faith rather than the *thing* that saves. What this also means is that, in my current understanding (5/2/2025), if a believer does not desire to be baptized, I would question the genuineness of said faith.

Interesting research questions:
**How have different theological understandings and practices of baptism across various Christian traditions shaped their broader understanding of God?**

### Suggested Research Questions to Further Explore the Topic:

1. **Theological Foundations**
    
    - How do different traditions define baptism (e.g., sacrament vs. ordinance), and how does this shape their view of God’s role in salvation?
    - What attributes of God (e.g., justice, mercy, sovereignty, grace) are emphasized in traditions that view baptism as necessary for salvation versus those that see it as symbolic?
2. **Biblical Interpretation**
    
    - How do different traditions interpret key baptismal passages (e.g., Acts 2:38, Romans 6:3-4, 1 Peter 3:21), and how do these interpretations reflect their broader theological framework?
    - How do traditions that emphasize believer’s baptism understand God's covenantal relationship differently from those that practice infant baptism?
3. **Historical and Cultural Influences**
    
    - How have historical developments (e.g., the Reformation, early church practices) shaped differing views on baptism and, consequently, the nature of God in these traditions?
    - How do cultural contexts influence whether baptism is seen as a personal act of faith or a communal, covenantal act?
4. **Practical and Devotional Implications**
    
    - How do different baptismal practices affect how believers experience and relate to God in their personal faith journeys?
    - In what ways do baptismal views shape a tradition’s understanding of Christian identity and spiritual formation?
